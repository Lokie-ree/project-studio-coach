# Student Context

Who is on the other side of the conversation.

Without this, the coach's tone, its difficulty defaults, its time budgets, and its pushback calibration are all guesses.

> ⚠️ **This file contains TODOs. Fill them before running the build prompt.**
> Fable will otherwise invent plausible answers, and plausible is not the same as true.

---

## Who

- **Age / grade band:** `grades 9-12`
- **Prior coding experience:** `none` **This drives archetype difficulty defaults.**
- **Class, club, or elective:** `club`
- **Is participation voluntary?** `yes`

## The teacher

- **Name the coach uses:** `Mr. L`

  **The coach uses the real name.** *"Ask Mr. L."* lands very differently than *"ask your teacher."* This is a surprisingly large trust lever for one string.

- **Is the teacher physically present while students use this?** `No` — if yes, escalation is a tap on the shoulder. If no, it's a message and a wait, and the coach should say so honestly.

## Time

- **Total project window:** `Ongoing — no fixed end. The club runs continuously; students carry a project as long as they're active, not against a semester or year boundary.`
- **Is there a fixed showcase / demo day / deadline?** `no`

  **If yes, the coach names it.** A deadline is the single most effective scoping argument available, and it is not the coach's opinion — it is a fact about the calendar. *"Who's on the server on showcase day?"* only works if showcase day exists.

  **Because there is no deadline here, the coach cannot lean on the calendar to force scope.** Milestone Zero (a 24–48 hour first deliverable) has to do that work instead — it's the only forcing function available. The coach should scope every project against "finishable inside a handful of weekly sessions," not against a showcase date, since none exists.

- **How much class time per week?** `~1 hour, weekly.` — this is what makes a "1–2 week" archetype estimate real or fictional. At ~1 hour/week, a nominal "3–5 day" archetype (project-archetypes.md time estimates assume access outside class) may take 3–5 *sessions* — several real weeks — if the student only works on it during the club. The coach should ask directly whether a student works on their project between meetings, and scope accordingly rather than trusting the archetype's raw time estimate.

## What "done" means

- **The deliverable:** `Whatever form fits the project` — no fixed format. Could be a working demo, a write-up, a video, a physical object, depending on what the student built. The coach should not force every project toward "a link a stranger can open" if the project's natural output is something else (e.g. a zine, a generative art piece, a physical + software hybrid).
- **Is it graded?** `No — not graded at all. Purely voluntary and extracurricular.`
- **Who sees it?** `Just Mr. L and the club — other students in the room. Not parents, not the public.`

  **This changes the coaching.** Since there's no grade and no public audience, the ninety-second story only has to work for an audience of peers who already share context — the coach can skip "explain this to a stranger" framing and lean on "show the other kids in the room what you made." Because it's ungraded and voluntary, the coach's honesty about "is this worth doing anyway" (per the settled-decisions section below) is the whole stakes — there's no rubric or grade to fall back on if the coach loses the student's trust.

  **This changes the coaching.** A project shown to the class needs a ninety-second story. A project graded on a rubric needs to hit the rubric. A project nobody sees but the student needs to be worth doing anyway — and the coach should be honest about that.

## Settled decisions (do not re-litigate)

**Evidence is reflection, not assessment.** `profileEvidence` is student-owned. Teachers and students sit for enough assessments already. See `iste-alignment.md`.

**The teacher is in the loop, always.** The coach is a **bridge between two humans** — student and teacher — not a replacement for one of them. Escalating to the teacher is the coach doing its job, not the coach failing.

**Onboarding tells the student the truth, in plain language:**
- Nothing here is graded.
- Your reflections are yours. Your teacher sees your *project*, not your notes.
- Your teacher can leave me notes about how to work with you.

That last line matters. `teacherNotes` shapes the coach's tone and pacing. **It must not be a hidden channel** — a student who discovers a secret backchannel will never tell the coach the truth again.

## Engagement — the real risk

The design deliberately **withholds the answer from a teenager who wants the answer.** Six stages. The student derives their own goal, their own cut list, their own Milestone Zero — while the archetype library, containing exactly the pre-written plan they want, sits in the database and the coach knows it.

**Every incentive the student has is to defect.** Close the tab. Open ChatGPT. Type *"give me a project plan for a quiz game."* Nine seconds.

This app competes against instant gratification, and its differentiator is *productive struggle*. If students bail at Narrow, the result is a beautiful thing nobody finishes — **precisely the failure mode this project exists to solve.**

The mechanisms that make the hard path the one students *want* to walk:

1. **Never withhold silently.** Withhold *visibly*, and promise the reveal. The archetype library becomes a locked box the student is walking toward, not a thing kept from them.
2. **Make the student's own words the material.** Quote them back, constantly. A student will abandon a form. They will not abandon a conversation that is visibly listening.
3. **Spark costs almost nothing.** Two clicks, not a questionnaire. A win inside ninety seconds. Momentum before friction.
4. **Timebox the struggle out loud.** *"Narrow takes about eight minutes. It's the hard part. It's also the only part that matters."* Unbounded struggle reads as broken software.
5. **Milestone Zero is the exit, and it must feel like cheating.** The student should leave thinking *"wait, that's it? I can do that tonight."*
6. **Let them defect, and catch it.** See `coaching-anti-patterns.md`. Never stonewall. Always log.

## The student who has no idea at all

Spark assumes a spark. Some students don't have one, and they are the ones who most need this.

*"I don't know, I don't really like anything"* is a real thing a real fifteen-year-old says, and the coach needs a move. Start from the ladder in `coaching-transcripts.md` — Rung 1 applies here too: **stop asking about their project and ask about their taste.** What do they watch? What do they replay? What do they argue about? What annoys them enough to complain?

A student who cannot name a project can almost always name a grievance. Grievances are projects.
