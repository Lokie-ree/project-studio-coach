# Milestone Zero Examples
A Milestone Zero is the smallest possible first deliverable — something a student can finish in 24–48 hours that proves the project is real and creates momentum.
 
A good Milestone Zero:
- Takes less than 2 hours of actual work
- Produces something you can look at, click, or share
- Doesn't require anything to be "finished" first
- Makes the next step obvious
A bad Milestone Zero is: "research the topic," "plan the project," or "set up my environment." Those are prerequisites, not milestones.
 
---
 
## Milestone Zeros by Project Type
 
---
 
### Games
 
**Platformer / Action Game**
→ Draw the main character (even stick figure), place it on a colored background, make it move left/right with arrow keys. No enemies, no levels — just a character you can control.
 
**Puzzle Game**
→ Build one puzzle — just one. It doesn't have a timer, score, or win screen yet. You just need to be able to interact with it and feel whether the mechanic is fun.
 
**Quiz Game**
→ Write 10 questions and answers in a Google Doc or text file. No interface yet. Just the content. Reading them out loud to a friend counts as testing.
 
**Text Adventure**
→ Write the opening room: a description, two choices, and what happens for each. Paste it into Twine or just a text file. That's it — one room, two branches.
 
**Card Game (digital)**
→ Define the 10 cards and what each one does, written in plain language. Lay them out in a Google Slides deck as rough card mockups with just text.

### Apps and Tools
 
**Habit Tracker**
→ Open a new Google Sheet. Add columns: Date, Habit, Done (Y/N), Notes. Manually log the past 3 days. See if it feels like the right structure.
 
**Recommendation Tool**
→ Write 5 possible recommendations and the 2–3 conditions that lead to each. Map it out in a flowchart on paper or in a Google Drawing — no code yet.
 
**Budget / Finance Tracker**
→ Create a spreadsheet with your categories (e.g., food, transport, fun). Enter last week's spending from memory. See if the categories feel right before building anything.
 
**Study Flashcard App**
→ Write 20 flashcards for one unit in a Google Sheet (front / back columns). Use them manually — flip between columns — before building any interface.
 
**Chatbot (rule-based or AI)**
→ Write 10 questions someone might ask your chatbot, and the ideal answer to each. This is your "training data" and your test script, and it reveals gaps before you write a line of code.
 
**Browser Extension**
→ Write a plain English description of: what page(s) it runs on, what it detects, what it changes. Then mock up a before/after screenshot in any image editor. If you can explain it to someone else, you can build it.
 
**Recipe Converter**
→ Manually convert one recipe from 4 servings to 7 servings. Note every unit you had to handle (cups, tsp, oz, etc.). That list of units is your coding spec.

### Data and Research
 
**Data Story / Visual Essay**
→ Find your dataset, download it, and open it in a spreadsheet. Make one chart — just one — and write two sentences about what it shows. If that sentence is interesting, the project is real.
 
**Research Write-Up + Artifact**
→ Write your main question and your tentative answer in 3 sentences. Find 3 sources that speak to it. That's your outline — you know what you're arguing and where the evidence is.
 
**"This Day In…" Digest**
→ Manually look up 5 interesting events that happened on today's date from Wikipedia or a history site. Paste them into a doc. Format them the way you'd want the final output to look — that's your template.
 
**Mapping Project**
→ Open Google My Maps. Add 5 pins for your topic (real or fictional). Give each a name and one sentence of description. See if the map tells a story yet.

### Creative and Design
 
**Portfolio / "About Me" Site**
→ Write the text for your homepage in a plain doc: one headline, 3 sentences about you, and 3 things you've made or want to make. That's your copy. The site can wait.
 
**Zine or Digital Magazine**
→ Write your table of contents: 4–6 pieces, each with a working title and one sentence describing what it's about. Pick the one piece you'd write first. Write 200 words of it.
 
**Generative Art**
→ Open the p5.js web editor. Run the default sketch. Change one number. Run it again. Change the color. The goal is just to make something move or draw — anything at all — in the first session.
 
**Mood Board / Aesthetic Archive**
→ Collect 12 images that fit your theme from royalty-free sources (Unsplash, Creative Commons). Drop them into a Google Slides deck in a rough grid. Show it to someone and see if they "get" the vibe.
 
**Font / Type Specimen Page**
→ Draw 5 letters of your font on paper. Scan or photograph them. Drop them in a doc next to each other. Do they feel like they belong to the same family? That test tells you if you have a concept.
 
**Tutorial / Video Series**
→ Record a 2-minute screen recording of just step 1 of whatever you're teaching. Don't edit it. Watch it back. Is it clear? That's your pilot episode.
 
**Animated Short**
→ Draw your character in 3 poses on paper: neutral, mid-action, end of action. Photograph them. Flip through the photos. That's your first animation test.
 
---
 
### Community and Advocacy
 
**Resource Directory**
→ Find and verify 10 real resources for your topic. Copy their name, website, and one-sentence description into a Google Doc. Formatted like a list. That's version 0 of your directory.
 
**Event or Club Landing Page**
→ Write the 5 things any visitor would want to know: What is this? Who is it for? When/where? How do I join? What's one thing that happened recently? That's your page structure and first draft of the copy.
 
**Petition / Advocacy Page**
→ Write the ask in one sentence. Write the "why it matters" in three sentences. If you can explain it that concisely, the page will be effective. Draft it before touching any design tools.

## The Milestone Zero Formula
 
When helping a student write theirs, use this template:
 
> **"By [day after tomorrow], I will have [specific tangible output] so that I can [what it lets you do next]."**
 
Examples:
- "By Thursday, I will have 10 quiz questions written in a doc so that I can start coding the interface."
- "By tomorrow night, I will have one room of my text adventure working in Twine so that I can test whether the mechanic feels right."
- "By Friday, I will have a spreadsheet with my data loaded and one chart made so that I can decide what story I'm telling."
---
 
## Signs a Milestone Zero is Too Small
- It's just installing software or creating an account — that's setup, not a milestone
- It produces nothing you can look at or share
## Signs a Milestone Zero is Too Large
- It would take more than a weekend to finish
- It requires another piece to be done first
- You'd feel "behind" if you didn't finish it in 48 hours
When in doubt, cut it in half. The point is to start, not to finish.
